# First-Year SIP Experience Research
## Consolidated Evidence Review of All 16 Tested Hypotheses

**Updated:** 6 November 2026  
**Scope:** First-year SIP experience, investor confidence, payment continuity, information-seeking behaviour, and product opportunities relevant to investment apps  
**Evidence sources:** Survey workbook (original and updated versions), five independent interviews (P01–P05), researcher-user interview (P00), and the AI Discovery Engine repository and outputs

---

## Executive summary

The strongest evidence continues to converge on three problem areas:

1. **Information clarity:** investors struggle to interpret performance terminology and judge whether returns are normal.
2. **Payment and mandate friction:** payment failures, missed debits, and unclear recovery paths create avoidable operational barriers.
3. **Early expectation mismatch:** the 3–6 month tenure group shows a pronounced expectation-versus-experience signal.

There is an important data-integrity qualification in this update. The new workbook contains 27 submissions and 26 responses that pass its current screening fields. However, the five newest entries were submitted within approximately 12 minutes and contain detailed narratives that closely mirror the existing P01–P05 interview cases. In addition, one earlier response that was recorded as **“No”** to the consent question in the previous workbook is now recorded as **“Yes, I agree.”**

Accordingly, this report uses two clearly separated views:

- **Core primary survey evidence (N=20):** the 20 eligible responses in the previous workbook, before the changed-consent record and five newest entries. This remains the conservative basis for the formal hypothesis verdicts and evidence-support ranking.
- **Updated workbook as-entered sensitivity (N=26):** the 26 responses that pass the current workbook's consent, age, and SIP-started fields. This is reported to show how the results change if every updated entry is treated as valid and independent. It is **provisional**, not silently substituted for the verified core sample.

The five newest records may be legitimate participant submissions, but their close alignment with P01–P05 means they should not be treated as independent corroboration without provenance confirmation. The consent reversal also needs a record of how consent was re-confirmed. Until those matters are resolved, the conservative N=20 analysis is the more defensible basis for external reporting.

### 1.3 Survey methodology and limitations

- The core survey contains 20 eligible SIP investors and the updated workbook-as-entered sensitivity contains 26.
- Both are small, descriptive samples; neither is a representative estimate of the Indian SIP market.
- The survey is mixed-platform. In the core sample, 6/20 respondents used INDmoney. In the updated workbook-as-entered set, 11/26 selected INDmoney. Results must not be described as INDmoney-only prevalence.
- Multi-select counts overlap and must not be summed as if they were mutually exclusive.
- The 3–6 month subgroup is small: n=5 in the core sample and n=7 in the updated workbook-as-entered sensitivity.
- A difference in counts between the dedicated payment-failure question and the separate issue checklist is retained rather than reconciled silently.
- “Not validated” means there is insufficient evidence in this study, not that the problem does not exist.

### 1.4 Interviews

**P01–P05** are five independent interview cases used in this research:

- **P01:** First-time INDmoney user, five months into SIPs. Expected visible growth by month three, struggled to understand XIRR, searched externally, encountered conflicting advice, and considered pausing.
- **P02:** First-time INDmoney user, nine months into SIPs. Paused a ₹5,000 SIP after freelance income fell and rent pressure increased. Found pause/cancel/mandate options unclear and did not receive a useful restart prompt.
- **P03:** First-time INDmoney user, three months into SIPs. Experienced a failed debit because of a UPI daily limit. The notification was generic; the participant manually retried.
- **P04:** First-time INDmoney user, ten months into SIPs. Goal-driven and confident; continued through market dips and did not seek external reassurance.
- **P05:** Groww user, five months into SIPs. Creator-driven expectations and conflicting online advice created uncertainty; a friend/forum helped reassure the participant. P05 is not an INDmoney user.

**P00** is the researcher-user interview: the researcher/project owner had used INDmoney for approximately three years and SIPs for approximately two years. P00 started with ₹500 split across three SIPs and later increased the total to ₹850. The participant deliberately chose an affordable amount, felt anxious when the portfolio was red but continued, experienced one failed debit attributed to insufficient funds, did not recall a clear advance notification, later noticed a reported ₹300 penalty, and wanted a one-day pre-debit reminder and clear, itemised explanations of costs and taxes.

P00 is useful contextual evidence but must remain separate from independent interview findings. It is not part of survey N=20 or N=26, and the reported ₹300 penalty is participant-reported rather than independently verified.

### 1.5 AI Discovery Engine

The AI Discovery Engine processed a broad, multi-source public corpus. The repository reports 8,179 ingested signals and 3,079 cleaned evidence points from five sources. Its ten themes and opportunity scores are discovery/prioritisation outputs, not survey prevalence estimates or validation scores.

The corpus includes app-store reviews, Reddit, ValuePickr, TradingQ&A, YouTube, and other sources. It is not restricted to INDmoney users or first-year SIP investors. Most records have unknown SIP tenure, so the engine cannot reliably establish first-year-specific prevalence.

The score implemented in `pipeline/stage3_cluster.py` is:

`0.30 × frequency + 0.30 × severity + 0.25 × actionability + 0.15 × recency`

This is an opportunity-prioritisation score, not a churn probability, validation score, or percentage of customers affected. The repository has a scoring-documentation inconsistency: a utility/default scoring implementation differs from the Stage 3 weighted-sum formula used for the displayed ranking. The displayed ranking matches the Stage 3 formula.

Other engine limitations identified in the repository review:

- The 20-item golden-set comparison is not an independent held-out production evaluation; it compares predefined human labels with predefined engine labels.
- Representative quotes are selected substantially by quote length rather than robust semantic relevance.
- Some quotes assigned to themes are weakly related, concern other platforms, or refer to general investing and unrelated app issues.
- Theme counts and corpus shares describe the collected corpus, not the proportion of customers experiencing a problem.

## 3. Evidence-support ranking

### 3.1 Ranking method

The ranking is an **ordinal assessment of evidence strength**, not a statistical score. It considers:

1. **Directness:** whether the survey measures the actual problem or only a nearby proxy.
2. **Convergence:** whether survey and independent interview evidence point in the same direction.
3. **Specificity:** whether concrete participant examples match the hypothesis.
4. **Counter-evidence:** whether important contradictory findings or alternative explanations exist.
5. **Source quality:** whether support comes from primary research or mainly from the AI Discovery Engine corpus.

The primary ranking below is based on the **core N=20 survey and independently conducted interviews**. The N=26 updated-workbook sensitivity generally strengthens the same leading themes, but does not change the ranking until the provenance of the six additional records is resolved.

### 3.4 Avoid double-counting overlapping hypotheses

The 16 entries are not 16 independent findings:

- **H1 and T2** describe substantially the same early expectation mismatch.
- **H2, P-H2, and T4** describe different layers of the payment/mandate friction problem: research-level causal hypothesis, product recovery opportunity, and engine theme.
- **H3 and T5** overlap around external information-seeking; T7 overlaps around peer/social influence.
- **T1 and T6** overlap around negative-return anxiety and checking behaviour.
- **P-H1 and P-H3** overlap around affordability and contribution adjustment.

The final presentation should group these into problem families rather than implying that each repeated label is a separate discovery.

## 4. What the updated workbook sensitivity changes

If all 26 responses in the updated workbook are treated as valid and independent, several descriptive signals become stronger:

## 5. Cross-hypothesis synthesis

### 5.1 Interpretation is the clearest underlying problem

In the core sample, 8/20 respondents reported confusing performance terminology and 8/20 were unsure whether returns were normal. Eleven respondents rated their confidence in understanding metrics at 1–3, and seven selected clearer return/volatility explanations as the best improvement.

The updated workbook sensitivity raises the corresponding figures to 11/26, 11/26, 16/26, and 9/26. This reinforces the direction of the signal, subject to the provenance caveat.

P01 struggled with XIRR; P00 relied on self-learning; P05 described uncertainty. P04 is an important counterexample.

**Synthesis:** A first-year investor may see a flat or negative portfolio without knowing whether it is normal, which metric to trust, or what action—if any—is appropriate. The clearest product opportunity is contextual, plain-language, source-backed performance explanation.

### 5.2 Payment friction is real, but its causal role in churn is not established

The dedicated payment question found 9/20 issues in the core sample and 11/26 in the updated workbook sensitivity. Interview examples include a UPI-limit failure (P03), mandate/control confusion during a cash-flow shock (P02), and unclear advance communication (P00).

In the core sample, 6/9 respondents with a payment issue considered changing their SIP, but so did 6/11 respondents without an issue. In the updated sensitivity, the comparison is 8/11 versus 8/14. Neither comparison proves that payment issues independently cause SIP discontinuation.

**Synthesis:** Payment and mandate friction is a substantiated operational problem. Improve debit visibility, explain specific failure reasons, confirm whether the SIP remains active, and provide actionable recovery steps. Do not claim a retention lift until it is measured.

### 5.3 Early expectation mismatch appears particularly salient in months 3–6

In the core 3–6 month cohort, all five respondents expected quick positive returns; four seriously considered a change, two actually changed course, and three named confusing/disappointing returns as the main reason. The updated sensitivity has seven people in this group, all seven selecting quick positive returns; six considered a change and five named confusing/disappointing returns as the main reason.

**Synthesis:** Early months may be a sensitive period when expectations meet actual market behaviour. This supports testing expectation-setting and return education during this period, but the cohort is too small to claim a universal “month 3–6 churn cliff.”

### 5.4 Affordability changes are a distinct retention use case

Ten of the core 20 respondents chose monthly affordability as the basis for their amount; the updated sensitivity is 15/26. P02's SIP became difficult after income fell and rent pressure increased. P00 deliberately chose a small amount to avoid missed debits.

**Synthesis:** A user can believe in long-term investing and still need to adjust contributions when cash flow changes. Reversible controls and clear restart options are plausible opportunities, but demand for a specific skip, step-down, or scheduled-restart feature has not been established.

### 5.5 External information-seeking is common; harmful influence is not established

All 20 core respondents selected at least one external source category; the updated sensitivity is 25/26. P01 and P05 described conflicting advice, while P04 did not need external help and P00 used online learning without relying on it to decide whether to continue through a red portfolio.

**Synthesis:** The opportunity is not to stop external information-seeking. It is to provide sufficiently clear and trustworthy explanations in-app so that users can interpret basic SIP performance. The evidence does not establish that external sources generally cause pauses.

## 7. Evidence gaps and next research steps

### 7.1 Improve survey measurement

1. **Separate payment events:** distinguish failed debit, mandate registration failure, UPI limit, insufficient balance, bank decline, and user-initiated cancellation.
2. **Measure recovery outcomes:** ask whether the user understood the reason, received a useful notification, retried successfully, missed an instalment, incurred a fee, or contacted support.
3. **Test flexible controls separately:** measure demand for skip, step-down, temporary pause, scheduled restart, and self-sizing rather than treating “pause/edit” as a proxy for all controls.
4. **Measure expectations precisely:** ask what return, time horizon, or visible progress the respondent expected and where the expectation came from. Do not infer a 15–20% annualised expectation from “quick positive returns.”
5. **Measure cross-selling and support directly:** ask about actual exposure, annoyance, support contact, resolution, and downstream behaviour.
6. **Clarify cost and tax context:** separate mutual-fund expense ratio, exit load, tax on gains, brokerage, payment penalties, and other product charges.
7. **Increase subgroup sizes:** recruit more first-year investors, particularly within months 3–6, and compare cohorts without overinterpreting small cells.
8. **Preserve consent and respondent provenance:** keep the original consent record, document any re-consent, and label simulated/test responses so they cannot be mixed with actual participant data.

### 7.2 Strengthen interview coverage

- Interview more users who actually stopped or paused SIPs, not only active continuers.
- Recruit across platforms and clearly tag platform, tenure, investor experience, and SIP status.
- Include people with multiple payment failures and people who recovered successfully.
- Interview users with changing income to understand which reversible controls they would trust.
- Include users who do not seek external information as a deliberate comparison group.

### 7.3 Test product interventions

- **Explanation intervention:** compare comprehension and confidence before and after a contextual XIRR/volatility explanation.
- **Debit communication intervention:** compare failure resolution and successful retry rates between existing notifications and reason-plus-next-step messaging.
- **Expectation-setting intervention:** measure whether early-tenure education improves understanding and reduces premature cancellation intent.
- **Flexible controls prototype:** test comprehension of pause, edit, and restart rules before testing behavioural outcomes.

Use behavioural outcomes—successful debit recovery, SIP continuation, restart rate, and comprehension—rather than relying only on stated feature preference.

## 9. Final conclusion

The most defensible overall conclusion is that first-year SIP continuity is shaped by a combination of **understanding, expectations, and operational reliability**.

The strongest core primary evidence points to:
- difficulty interpreting performance and deciding whether returns are normal;
- payment and mandate friction, particularly when the reason for failure or next step is unclear; and
- an expectation-versus-experience gap in the small 3–6 month subgroup.

The updated workbook, if all new records are ultimately verified as independent, strengthens these same signals. Until then, the N=26 figures should remain a sensitivity analysis rather than replace the core N=20 evidence base. The consent-status reversal and the five newest scenario-like submissions should be resolved before presenting the expanded number as a verified respondent count.

The research also identifies meaningful secondary opportunities around cash-flow flexibility and trustworthy information-seeking. However, the evidence does not prove that flexible controls, one-tap recovery, or in-app advice will improve retention. Those are product hypotheses to prototype and test.

The AI Discovery Engine was valuable for surfacing a broad opportunity landscape, but its theme scores and corpus shares should be used for discovery and prioritisation—not as customer prevalence or validation statistics.

**Recommended deck narrative:** lead with Information Clarity, Payment Recovery, and Early Expectation Mismatch. Present Flexible Controls and External Information-Seeking as evidence-backed opportunities requiring feature validation. Keep Cross-Selling Fatigue, Hidden Charges/Tax Complexity, and Support Resolution Gap in the future-research backlog until stronger primary evidence is collected.
